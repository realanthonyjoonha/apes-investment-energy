- Behind-the-meter gas generation: ~40 projects / 48 GW announced, why hyperscalers are bypassing the grid entirely
- Spark spread analysis: how to calculate profitability of gas-fired generation at different gas prices and power prices
- Texas as ground zero: ~58 GW of gas power in planning/construction, more than every country except China

## Module 6: Nuclear Power Fundamentals
Status: NOT STARTED
- How nuclear fission power generation works (simplified): fuel assemblies, reactor types (PWR, BWR), steam generation, turbine-generator, cooling systems
- Nuclear's unique value proposition: 24/7 carbon-free baseload power, 90%+ capacity factors (vs. 25-35% for solar/wind), 60-80 year plant life
- The existing U.S. fleet: 93 operating reactors, ~95 GW capacity, providing ~19% of U.S. electricity and ~47% of carbon-free electricity
- Nuclear cost structure: high upfront capital cost, very low marginal operating cost ($25-30/MWh all-in for existing plants), fuel costs only 5-10% of total
- Why nuclear is ranked #5 near-term but #1 long-term: execution risk on new builds is severe, but existing fleet economics are extraordinary
- The nuclear-DC convergence: why hyperscalers want nuclear — 24/7 baseload matches AI training loads perfectly, carbon-free meets ESG commitments
- Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) for nuclear: how Amazon/Microsoft/Google/Meta are contracting directly with nuclear plant operators
- Nuclear regulation: NRC's role, operating licenses, safety oversight, the difference between license renewal (straightforward) and new construction (grueling)
- Life extensions: NRC approved 13 license renewals in 2025 (12,000 MW preserved), Turkey Point holds first 80-year license (to 2052/2053)
- Executive order context: target quadrupling U.S. nuclear capacity to 400 GW by 2050 — what this means for policy and investment
- Morgan Stanley's $2.2T nuclear investment estimate through 2050, IEA projects annual nuclear investment rising from $70B to $210B by 2035
- The policy advantage: OBBBA preserved nuclear credits (45U) through 2031 while killing wind/solar credits — nuclear has the strongest policy tailwind

## Module 9: Nuclear Fuel Supply Chain
Status: NOT STARTED
- The nuclear fuel cycle: mining → milling → conversion → enrichment → fabrication → reactor → spent fuel storage
- Uranium mining: who produces it and where — Kazakhstan (Kazatomprom, ~43% of global supply), Canada (Cameco), Australia, Namibia, U.S. (minimal current production)
- Cameco (CCJ) deep-dive: world's largest publicly traded uranium miner, McArthur River/Cigar Lake (highest-grade deposits on Earth), Westinghouse fuel fabrication acquisition, vertically integrated
- Uranium market dynamics: spot prices $63-83/lb in 2025 → above $100/lb early March 2026, long-term contracts at $90/lb (14-year highs), Cameco contract ceilings $140-150/lb
- Goldman Sachs supply/demand forecast: ~17,500-ton deficit by 2030, growing to ~100,000 tons by 2045 — structural scarcity
- The HALEU bottleneck — the most critical nuclear constraint:
  - High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (5-20% enrichment) required by most advanced reactor designs
  - U.S. has virtually no commercial-scale production
  - Centrus Energy (LEU) Piketon, Ohio: only domestic HALEU source, produced ~920 kg vs. projected demand of 50 metric tons/year by 2035
  - DOE committed $2.7B over 10 years (Jan 2026) to expand capacity — contracts to Centrus, Urenco USA, Orano, General Matter
  - Major new capacity won't arrive until early 2030s — already pushed TerraPower Natrium from 2028 to 2030
- Centrus Energy (LEU) deep-dive: sole domestic HALEU producer, DOE contracts, enrichment technology (centrifuge), pathway from 920 kg to commercial scale
- BWX Technologies (BWXT) deep-dive: sole U.S. naval nuclear reactor manufacturer, "foundry for SMRs," nuclear fuel and components, benefits from any reactor construction regardless of design
- Russian enrichment dependency: historically ~24% of U.S. enrichment supply came from Russia, sanctions and the uranium import ban creating supply chain restructuring
- Enrichment capacity expansion: Centrus, Urenco USA (New Mexico), Orano (potential U.S. facility) — timeline and investment requirements
- How uranium price dynamics differ from other commodities: long-term contracts dominate (vs. spot for oil/gas), utility procurement cycles, inventory overhang vs. structural deficit
- Investment framework for nuclear fuel: CCJ as the uranium price play, LEU as the HALEU optionality play, BWXT as the picks-and-shovels play

## Module 10: Renewables in a Post-IRA World
Status: NOT STARTED
- What OBBBA (One Big Beautiful Bill Act, July 4, 2025) changed for renewables:
  - Solar/wind credits (45Y/48E): terminate for facilities placed in service after Dec 31, 2027; construction must begin by July 2026
  - This creates a compressed rush (now through July 2026) followed by a demand cliff
  - Clean hydrogen credits (45V): terminated for construction after Dec 31, 2025
  - Clean vehicle credits: eliminated after Sept 30, 2025
- What survived: 45X advanced manufacturing credits (to 2032), nuclear credits (45U through 2031), 45Q carbon capture (enhanced)
- The July 2026 deadline: how the construction-start requirement creates a land rush for solar/wind projects NOW, and what happens to demand after
- First Solar (FSLR) deep-dive: only at-scale domestic solar manufacturer, 14 GW capacity by 2026, 45X credit monetization ($1.5B in 2025), 45.6% gross margins, insulated from Chinese panel tariffs (175%+)
- Why First Solar wins regardless of policy: domestic manufacturing advantage, 45X survival to 2032, tariff protection, differentiated CdTe thin-film technology
- U.S. solar manufacturing boom: capacity surged from 8 GW pre-IRA to 56.5 GW by mid-2025 (700%+ increase) — but post-OBBBA overcapacity risk
- Chinese solar panel dynamics: 175%+ cumulative tariffs, domestic content requirements, supply chain decoupling
- Offshore wind: effectively frozen — Trump suspended all lease permitting Jan 2025, OBBBA eliminated key wind credits
- Hyperscaler renewable procurement despite policy headwinds: Microsoft 34.7 GW contracted (world's largest buyer), Google Pine Island 1.9 GW deal (wind + solar + storage)
- McKinsey projection: 65-85 GW dispatchable clean generation needed just for hyperscaler 24/7 commitments by 2030
- The "renewables + storage" model: how pairing solar/wind with 4-hour batteries changes the dispatchability problem
- Investment implications: First Solar as the standout survivor, the rush-then-cliff dynamic creating entry points, how to position around the July 2026 deadline

- The cost revolution: battery pack costs for stationary storage hit $70/kWh in 2025 (45% YoY decline) — solar+storage now competitive with gas peakers
- How battery storage works in the grid: peak shaving, frequency regulation, renewable firming, energy arbitrage, capacity market participation
- Geographic concentration: California, Texas, Arizona = ~82% of new additions — why these markets and what it means for grid reliability
- Tesla Energy deep-dive: 39% North American BESS market share, 31.4 GWh deployed in 2024, Megapack product line, how to evaluate Tesla Energy separately from the auto business
- SEIA projection: annual installations exceeding 47 GW / 110 GWh by 2030 — growth trajectory
- Chinese BESS competitive threat: 7 of top 10 global integrators are China-based, 82%+ cumulative tariffs by Jan 2026 — tariffs vs. cost advantage
- ESS Tech (GWH) as a cautionary tale: going-concern warning, $0.8M cash — the risk profile of early-stage storage companies
- Long-Duration Energy Storage (LDES) — the next frontier:
  - Form Energy iron-air technology: 300 MW / 30 GWh Google/Xcel deal (world's largest battery project by energy capacity), Minnesota DC phases 2028-2031, 100-hour discharge duration
  - Hydrostor: $1.76B DOE commitment for 500 MW / 4 GWh compressed air in California
  - Why LDES matters for data centers: multi-day backup, seasonal storage, reducing gas dependency for 24/7 power
- LDES funding reality check: overall funding declined 30% YoY in 2025, VC investment fell 72% — early stage with uncertain economics
- The 4-hour vs. 100-hour storage distinction: short-duration (Li-ion) is proven and scaling; long-duration is pre-commercial but transformative if it works
- How storage fits into the five-pillar framework: ranked #4 because battery cost declines are real but renewables policy cliff limits the pairing opportunity near-term
